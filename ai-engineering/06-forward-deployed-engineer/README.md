# Part 6 · The Forward Deployed Engineer

> The skill that turns everything in Parts 0–5 into a career: shipping it *for someone else*, in
> *their* environment, against *their* definition of success.
> [← back to the track index](../README.md) · [ROADMAP Weeks 11–12](../ROADMAP.md#phase-6--the-forward-deployed-engineer-weeks-11-12--part-6)

An FDE is not a better prompt engineer. **FDE = strong AI engineer × consultant instincts.** You
already have the first half if you've made it through Parts 0–5: you can build an agent, eval it,
trace it, secure it, ship it. The second half — the part most engineers never train — is knowing
*which workflow to point that machinery at*, how to earn trust from people who didn't ask for an
AI project, and how to hand off something that survives after you leave the room. A junior AI
engineer optimizes a model. An FDE optimizes a customer's outcome, and the model is one of the
tools they use to get there.

The three chapters here are the three phases of every real engagement, in order:

| Chapter | The question it answers |
|---|---|
| [24 · Discovery & Scoping](24-discovery-and-scoping.md) | Which workflow is actually worth automating, and how do you scope a POC that's narrow enough to demo in two weeks but real enough that success is undeniable? |
| [25 · The Demo-Driven POC](25-the-demo-driven-poc.md) | How do you go from a first meeting to a working pilot, and what do you do when the agent fails *live*? |
| [26 · POC → Production](26-poc-to-production.md) | What has to change for an enterprise to trust this in production, and who owns it after you leave? |

**The habit for this part:** everything from Parts 0–5 becomes evidence in a conversation with a
skeptical buyer — the eval scorecard, the trace, the injection results, the cost math. An FDE never
says "trust me," they say "here's the number, here's how I got it, here's how you can check it
yourself." This part is where you learn to hand that evidence to someone who isn't an engineer and
still have it land.
